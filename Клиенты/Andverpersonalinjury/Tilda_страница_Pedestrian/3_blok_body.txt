          <div class="ldvp__card ldvp__card--note"><b>&ldquo;He came out of nowhere.&rdquo;</b><p>That is the driver's version, not a legal conclusion. Drivers are expected to watch for people on foot. Who was at fault depends on what happened: where you were, signals, speed, lighting, witnesses and video.</p></div>
          <div class="ldvp__card ldvp__card--note"><b>&ldquo;You weren't in the crosswalk.&rdquo;</b><p>Being outside a crosswalk does not automatically end a claim. A pedestrian outside a crosswalk generally has to yield to close vehicles, and a driver still has to use due care. How much that matters depends on the facts.</p></div>
          <div class="ldvp__card ldvp__card--note"><b>&ldquo;We just need a quick recorded statement,&rdquo; or &ldquo;Here is a quick offer.&rdquo;</b><p>If you sign a release, the claim may be closed before the full medical picture is known. You can find out where you stand before you agree to anything.</p></div>
          <div class="ldvp__card ldvp__card--note"><b>&ldquo;The driver drove away.&rdquo;</b><p>That does not necessarily end a claim. Video, witnesses and the police report can sometimes help identify the driver, and other coverage may apply, including your own auto policy. Report the crash to the police as soon as you can: policies and the law can set short deadlines.</p></div>
        </div>

  <section class="ldvp__sec ldvp__sec--white" id="ldv-checklist">
    <div class="ldvp__in">
      <p class="ldvp__eyebrow">First days</p>
      <h2 class="ldvp__h2">After being hit by a car: what to do now, and what to hold off on</h2>
      <div class="ldvp__cols">
        <div class="ldvp__col">
          <h3>Do now</h3>
          <ul class="ldvp__ticks">
            <li>Get medical care. Tell the doctor everything that hurts, even if it seems small. Some pain shows up hours or days later.</li>
            <li>Make sure the police are called and a report is made. Save the report number.</li>
            <li>Take photos if you can: the crosswalk or street, signals and signs, lighting, your injuries, and the vehicle and its plate.</li>
            <li>Write down names and phone numbers of witnesses. Notice nearby cameras (stores, doorbells): footage may be deleted after some time.</li>
            <li>Keep the clothes, shoes, phone, bag and glasses you had on as they are. Do not wash, repair or throw them away.</li>
          </ul>
        </div>
        <div class="ldvp__col">
          <h3>Hold off until you have talked to an attorney</h3>
          <ul class="ldvp__nots">
            <li>Giving a recorded statement to the driver's insurer.</li>
            <li>Signing a release or accepting a quick offer.</li>
            <li>Saying &ldquo;it was my fault&rdquo; or &ldquo;I'm fine&rdquo; at the scene or to an insurer. Say what hurts and what you remember.</li>
            <li>Posting about the crash on social media.</li>
          </ul>
        </div>
      </div>
      <p class="ldvp__note">This is general information, not legal advice.</p>
    </div>

      <p class="ldvp__eyebrow">Frequently asked questions</p>
      <h2 class="ldvp__h2">Questions about pedestrian accidents and hiring a lawyer</h2>
      <details><summary>The driver says I stepped out in front of them. Does that end my claim?</summary>
        <p>No. That is the driver's version, and it is not a legal conclusion. California law generally requires drivers to yield to pedestrians in a marked crosswalk or in an unmarked crosswalk at an intersection, and says drivers must still use due care. It also says a pedestrian may not suddenly leave a curb and walk into the path of a vehicle that is too close to yield. What really happened depends on where you were, signals, speed, lighting, witnesses and video. If a pedestrian is found partly responsible, California uses comparative fault: damages can be reduced by that percentage, not automatically denied. A free case review looks at what happened in your crash.</p></details>
      <details><summary>Can I have a claim if I was not in a crosswalk, or was jaywalking?</summary>
        <p>Possibly. Outside a crosswalk, a pedestrian generally has to yield to vehicles that are close enough to be an immediate hazard, but California law also says drivers must still use due care toward people on the road. An insurer may argue that you were partly at fault. That can reduce compensation by your percentage of fault; it does not automatically cancel the claim. Tell the attorney exactly where you crossed and what you saw.</p></details>
      <details><summary>The driver left the scene. What can I do?</summary>
        <p>Leaving the scene after hitting a person is a crime in California, because the driver must stop. It does not necessarily end your claim. Police reports, witnesses and nearby camera footage can sometimes help identify the driver, and other coverage may apply, such as the uninsured motorist coverage in your own auto policy. Policies and the law can set short deadlines, for example for reporting the crash to the police, so report it as soon as you can and keep the report number. An attorney can review what may apply to you.</p></details>
      <details><summary>I wasn't driving. Whose insurance pays for my care?</summary>
        <p>It depends on the facts. Depending on the case, coverage may come from the driver's auto liability policy, from your own auto policy, or from other sources. Do not assume that something is or is not available. The review looks at who may be responsible and what coverage may exist.</p></details>
      <details><summary>The other driver's insurance company called me. What should I do?</summary>
        <p>First identify who is calling and which insurer they represent. Avoid guessing about facts you do not know, minimizing injuries that are still being evaluated, or signing releases you do not understand. If you are unsure about a recorded statement or an offer, consider getting legal advice before agreeing to it.</p></details>
      <details><summary>Can the city or county be responsible for a dangerous crossing?</summary>
        <p>Sometimes. If a road or crossing condition, such as a broken signal or poor lighting, contributed to the crash, a claim against a government agency may be possible. Those claims can require a notice much earlier than a regular lawsuit, generally within six months of the injury. Whether this applies to your case depends on the facts.</p></details>
      <details><summary>How much does a pedestrian accident lawyer cost?</summary>
        <p>The initial case review is free, and there is no fee unless we win. Landver Law handles personal-injury matters on a contingency basis, advances litigation costs and collects its legal fee when a recovery is obtained. Exact financial terms are confirmed in the written attorney-client agreement before representation begins.</p></details>
      <details><summary>How much is my pedestrian accident case worth?</summary>
        <p>No honest number can be named without the facts. It depends on your injuries, medical costs, time off work, future care, who was at fault and the insurance coverage available. The review shows what applies to your case. Landver Law does not promise a result.</p></details>
      <details><summary>My treatment is still ongoing. Is it too early to contact a lawyer?</summary>
        <p>No. You do not have to wait until treatment is finished before asking for a case review. In a serious injury case, the eventual medical outcome may still be uncertain. The important starting point is understanding what happened, what treatment has occurred so far and what evidence may need to be preserved.</p></details>
      <details><summary>How long do I have to bring a pedestrian injury claim in California?</summary>
        <p>For many California personal-injury lawsuits, the general deadline is two years from the date of injury. But that is not a universal rule. Different facts can create different deadlines. If a government agency or a road condition is involved, a claim against a California government agency can require an administrative claim much earlier, generally within six months of the injury. Claims under your own insurance policy, for example for a hit-and-run, can have their own deadlines. Do not use the general two-year period to assume your own deadline.</p></details>
