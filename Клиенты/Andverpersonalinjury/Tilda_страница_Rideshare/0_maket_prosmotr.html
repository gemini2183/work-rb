After the crash

What Uber, Lyft and their insurers may say after a crash

“Whose insurance pays?”

It can depend on what the driver was doing in the app when the crash happened: logged off, waiting for a request, driving to a pickup, or carrying a passenger. California sets insurance requirements for rideshare companies and drivers, and they differ by stage. Rules have changed in recent years, so this page does not list amounts.

“The driver wasn't on a trip.”

Which insurance applies can depend on app status, but it does not by itself decide whether you have a claim. The trip record, your receipt and screenshots can help show what the driver was doing.

“Just give a recorded statement and sign here.”

A recorded statement or a release can limit a claim before the full medical picture is known. You can ask for time and get advice first.

“The driver is an independent contractor.”

Rideshare companies describe their drivers this way. Who may be responsible in your case depends on the facts: the driver, another driver, the company, or more than one.

If you were partly at fault, California reduces compensation by your percentage of fault. It does not automatically cancel it, and the insurer's first number is not always the final one.

First days

After an Uber or Lyft crash: what to do now, and what to hold off on

Do now

  • Get medical care. Tell the doctor everything that hurts, even if it seems small.
  • Save screenshots of the trip details, your receipt, the driver's name, the car and license plate, and any messages in the app.
  • Take photos of the scene and the vehicles if you can, and save the police report number.
  • Write down names and phone numbers of witnesses and the insurance information of every driver involved, including any rideshare insurance.

Hold off until you have talked to an attorney

  • Giving a recorded statement to any insurer, including the one for Uber or Lyft.
  • Signing a release or accepting a quick payment, including one offered through the app.
  • Deleting the app, messages or ride history.
  • Posting about the crash on social media.

This is general information, not legal advice.

Frequently asked questions

Questions about Uber and Lyft accidents and hiring a lawyer

Who pays after an Uber or Lyft accident: the driver, Uber or Lyft, or my own insurance?

It can depend on what the driver was doing in the app when the crash happened: logged off, logged on and waiting for a request, driving to pick someone up, or carrying a passenger. California sets insurance requirements for rideshare companies and drivers, and they differ by stage. Other drivers' insurance and your own coverage may also matter. Coverage rules have changed in recent years, so we do not list amounts here. A free case review looks at what applies in your crash.

I was a passenger. Who can I make a claim against?

Possibly more than one party. The question is usually who caused the crash: the rideshare driver, another driver, or both. Whether the rideshare company can also be responsible depends on the facts. Rideshare companies describe their drivers as independent contractors, and that can affect the claim. Tell us what happened and the attorney will explain what may apply to you.

The driver says the app was off. Does that end my claim?

No, not by itself. App status can change which insurance may apply, and the driver's personal policy may come into play. The trip record, your receipt and screenshots can help show what the driver was doing. Save them, and do not delete the app.

Uber's or Lyft's insurance company called me. What should I do?

First identify who is calling and which insurer they represent. Avoid guessing about facts you do not know, playing down injuries that are still being evaluated, or signing releases you do not understand. If you are unsure about a recorded statement or a quick payment, consider getting legal advice before agreeing to it.

I was in another car, on foot or on a bike when a rideshare driver hit me. Do I have a claim?

Possibly. You do not need to have been a passenger. Who is responsible depends on what happened and what the driver was doing at the time. Bring what you have: the police report number, photos, witnesses and the driver's insurance information.

What if I was partly at fault?

It does not automatically end a claim. California uses comparative fault: damages can be reduced by your percentage of responsibility, not automatically denied. How much it matters depends on what happened. Tell the attorney the whole story, including the parts you are unsure about.

What should I save from the ride?

Screenshots of the trip details and route, your receipt, the driver's name, the car and license plate, and messages in the app. Also photos, the police report number, witness contacts and your medical records and bills. If you are unsure whether something matters, keep it.

My treatment is still ongoing. Is it too early to contact a lawyer?

No. You do not have to wait until treatment is finished before asking for a case review. In a serious injury case, the eventual medical outcome may still be uncertain. The important starting point is understanding what happened, what treatment has occurred so far and what evidence may need to be preserved.

How long do I have to bring an Uber or Lyft injury claim in California?

For many California personal-injury lawsuits, the general deadline is two years from the date of injury. But that is not a universal rule. Different facts can create different deadlines. For example, if a government agency or a road condition is involved, a claim against a California government agency can require an administrative claim much earlier, generally within six months of the injury. Do not use the general two-year period to assume your own deadline.
